Five things to know

  1. It is big and it is now

    A very strong El Niño, likely the strongest on record. Peak October–December 2026, lasting into early 2027.

  2. Same event, opposite problems

    Drought in Southern Africa, Central America, Southeast Asia and the Pacific. Floods on the Peru–Ecuador coast and in East Africa.

  3. Plans exist, money does not

    Many appeals are 55–80% unfunded. Local groups get money 3–6 months late, through middlemen.

  4. The real gap is the last step

    Forecasts are good. Warnings fail to become decisions by a named person in a district or village, in their language, in time.

  5. Where we fit

    Facilitation, local-language translation, matchmaking needs with offers, and moving Nordic money to local groups. Norad's new civil society grants open for feedback in December.

The ocean and the air, in numbers RP

  • Fastest rise on record. The weekly Niño-3.4 reading hit +3.0 to +3.05 °C by mid-September, climbing faster than in any earlier event. Off the South American coast (Niño 1+2) it stayed above +4 °C, causing marine heatwaves.
  • Fuel in the tank. Water 50–150 m down is +8 to +10 °C above normal. That heat keeps the surface warm through the 2026–27 winter.
  • The air has flipped too. The Southern Oscillation Index fell to −14.6 in August and −20.1 by mid-September. The Pacific trade winds have weakened or reversed. Scientists call this a collapse of the Walker Circulation.
  • Australia confirmed it. The Bureau of Meteorology declared El Niño on 16 June 2026. On 27 September its index read +2.72 °C, the second-highest weekly value since 2008.
  • Indian Ocean Dipole: +0.76 °C in late September, peaking October–November. Together with El Niño it makes southern Australia and Southern Africa hotter and drier, and the Horn of Africa wetter.
  • New baseline. This event sits on top of record global ocean heat. Past events (1997, 2015) may not predict it well.

Forgotten impacts

The second-order effects. These are where small, focused help is rare.

  • HEALTH Cholera follows drought (188k cases in southern Africa, 2024). Dengue follows heat and rain (13M cases in the Americas, 2024). Haze from fires (~100k early deaths, Indonesia 2015).
  • POWER Hydropower dams run dry. Then clinics, water pumps, cold chains and phone-based warnings fail too.
  • PRICES Export bans (India rice 2023), cocoa and coffee spikes, Panama Canal limits.
  • PROTECTION Child marriage, school dropout and gender-based violence rise in drought. UN Women issued a gender alert for Asia-Pacific (Sep 2026).
  • MENTAL HEALTH Farmer suicide rises with drought months. Youth trauma after floods.
  • CONFLICT & MIGRATION New civil conflict risk in the tropics doubles in El Niño years. Dry seasons push migration from Central America.
  • FROST RP Dry soil loses heat fast at night. That brings hard ground frost to highland and temperate farms, killing flowering crops before drought is even declared (PNG highlands, Australian cereals).
  • HEAT RP ICPAC issued its first extreme-heat early warnings in August 2026 to prevent heat deaths.
  • GLOBAL SUPPLY RP Peru's anchoveta fishery collapses in marine heatwaves, cutting global fishmeal and fertiliser. Drought hits coffee, cocoa and sugar. Low water in Panama's Gatun Lake limits ship depth and daily transits, raising freight rates.
  • HERDERS RP Drought pushes herders' routes into farmland in the Sahel, Lake Chad and Liptako-Gourma. Competition over water and grazing triggers violence and more displacement.
  • INSURANCE Payout triggers can miss real losses. Malawi 2016: the model assumed a maize variety farmers no longer grew.

Lessons from past El Niños

  • Warnings were not the problem. Action was. Horn of Africa 2011: months of warning, action only after the rains failed.
  • Interpreters decided who acted. 2015–16: groups with someone to turn forecasts into decisions acted early.
  • Early pays. FAO 2023–24: about $7 saved per $1 spent early. Cash before a flood beat the same cash after.
  • Language matters. After Cyclone Idai, over 40% of affected people could not read the Portuguese warnings.
  • 2026: the delay is a choice. RP The climate signal was stable from February 2026. The Red Cross Climate Centre (28 Sep): the forecast is as good as forecasting gets; every day of delay is an institutional choice, not a gap in knowledge.
  • False starts kill seed. RP In 2015–16 and 2023–24 thousands of farmers planted after early, isolated rains and lost their seed when the dry spell came back. 2026 advisories in Southern Africa now focus on not planting too early.
  • Money leaves too soon. RP Funding surges during relief and disappears in early recovery, leaving communities exposed to the next shock.
  • Outsiders can harm. Local groups get under 10% of humanitarian money (3.8% directly). New middlemen, parallel warning channels and careless data use make it worse.

Unknown unknowns RP

Risks that standard El Niño preparedness tends to miss.

  1. Record ocean heat underneath

    This El Niño sits on top of record global ocean temperatures from 2025–26. Past events may not predict how the atmosphere responds, so expect compound extremes no model shows.

  2. Frost from dry soil

    Assessments watch daytime heat and rain totals. Dry soil loses heat at night, so hard frost can wipe out flowering crops before drought is declared.

  3. Debt crisis removes the buffer

    Governments in debt distress cannot pay food, fuel and fertiliser subsidies. Price shocks then land directly on households.

  4. AI data centres compete for water

    New energy- and water-hungry data centres near dry cities compete with people for groundwater during drought. Nobody has modelled this.

  5. Green grids fail twice

    Drought cuts hydropower while heat and haze cut solar output and power-line capacity, at the same time.

  6. Grain export bans

    Geopolitical tension makes sudden bans on rice, wheat or maize more likely at the first sign of a poor harvest, driving fast global price spikes.

  7. Climate and conflict feed each other

    In the Sahel, Lake Chad and eastern DR Congo, drought pushes herders into farmland, triggering violence. Armed groups recruit displaced youth.

The biggest need after money: turning warnings into action

The forecasts are good. Most of the deadliest weather disasters this century were forecast in advance. The failure happens in the last step: a warning must become a decision by a named person in a district or village, in time.

Why warnings do not become action

  • Fear of acting in vain. Blame for acting on a wrong forecast is bigger than credit for acting early. Horn of Africa 2011: famine warning in March, money in July. Same pattern again in 2022.
  • Nobody has the written right to decide. Plans name agencies, not people. A forecast does not legally trigger an order. Reported example: Nepal floods, 26 Aug 2026. The weather office had no authority to order evacuation; community phone calls saved 900 students. (single source, verify)
  • Plans stop at national level. Policies exist but local authorities lack capacity to run them (REAP 2023–24).
  • Money is released late. Kenya's drought contingency funds are often released after the situation gets worse.
  • The forecast is not farm advice. RP "75% chance of below-normal rain" does not tell a farmer when to plant, which seed to choose (short-season, drought-tolerant sorghum) or how to avoid a false start.
  • Wrong language, wrong channel. RP Forecasts go out in English, Spanish or French on websites. Remote, indigenous and pastoralist people listen to community radio in local languages.
  • Local reality is invisible. RP Big climate models miss frost hollows in valleys, falling groundwater and local grain price spikes. Local actors lack simple tools to link weather, market prices and malnutrition.
  • Rigid triggers pay too late. RP Some funds need several weather thresholds confirmed before money moves. By then the dry spell has already damaged young crops.
  • Local groups cannot reach the money. RP Trusted community and faith groups fail strict compliance and audit rules for pooled funds.
  • The warning does not land. 41% of Cyclone Idai survivors could not read the Portuguese warnings. Standard forecast charts are read correctly only 57% of the time. In Nepal, 51% of women got warnings by word of mouth. 86% of people with disabilities feel left out of disaster planning.

1 · ThresholdA written rule. Albay, Philippines: evacuate at ≥120 km/h winds and a direct-hit forecast. ~10 years with zero deaths.
2 · Local deciderA named person with authority. Bangladesh: 76,000 volunteers, half women, own warning and evacuation.
3 · Existing channelMoney or delivery that already runs. Mozambique and Ethiopia used existing safety nets to pay early.
4 · RehearsalPractise the decision. Nepal Karnali gauge readers phone downstream: lead time 2–3 h → 7–8 h.

Pitfalls to avoid

  • Big per diems attract the wrong people and "workshops for the allowance".
  • Workshop fatigue is often a signal of unequal partnership, not apathy.
  • Token participation that ignores local and indigenous knowledge.
  • Never run a session without a named decision owner or funder for the actions it produces. Give results back within 2 weeks.
  • Using indigenous knowledge without free, prior and informed consent.

Why platforms like this usually stall

  • Data goes stale. Even HDX's curated crisis data lost 6 points of freshness in one year. A volunteer-filled map decays faster.
  • Pilots never become real use. Most Ushahidi deployments were tests or training.
  • Parallel tools get ignored. Haiti 2010: volunteer data flooded in, but responders could not use it because it ran outside the official system.
  • Community-owned lasts, outsider-built fades. Start Network's CLIP programme: 74% of community-led solutions reached a scaling path.
  • Local groups value contacts most. A Start Network hub leader named "the ease of getting in touch with other organisations" as the key value.

The first thing to test: a matchmaking service done by people

A local group posts one need, for example "drought cash design help, Malawi, Chichewa". Within 7 days we introduce them to someone who can help. Success = a call actually happens.

  1. Week 1Pick one region (Southern Africa drought or Central America Dry Corridor). Contact 30 local groups through Start Network hubs and NEAR, plus 20 people offering help. Collect needs and offers with a simple form. Register for the Berlin Dialogue Platform.
  2. Week 2Put them in a spreadsheet as "need / offer cards". Make 10 introductions by hand (email, WhatsApp). Publish a simple map at province level only.
  3. Week 3Follow up. Count calls held and next steps agreed. Test AI search over the cards plus Anticipation Hub documents, inside the test group only.
  4. Week 4Interview 10 participants. Continue only if 5 of 10 introductions led to a call and 3 local groups want to renew their card. Take results to the Anticipation Hub and Norad as a partnership request.

Stop rule: if nobody renews their card after 60 days, stop. Cards expire after 60 days unless renewed; this keeps the data fresh.

Do-no-harm rules for any map

  • No individuals. No locations more precise than district level.
  • No protection, gender-violence, ethnic or displacement data.
  • Organisations opt in. Contact goes through a relay, not public emails. Clear takedown route.
  • AI searches public documents only, never personal data.
  • Use existing standards (HXL, IATI, OpenStreetMap boundaries) and link to existing tools instead of copying them.

Guides: ICRC data protection handbook, Signal Code (Harvard Humanitarian Initiative), OCHA data responsibility guidelines.

Lessons from earlier open projects

  • OpenStreetMap works because contributors are spread out but the data model is one shared database.
  • HDX works because OCHA curates it and pays a team. It is a central catalogue, not true federation.
  • GeoNode federation ended as islands of agency portals with patchy upkeep.
  • Sahana faded once volunteer maintenance stopped. Ushahidi survived with a hosted service and grants.
  • Open Referral shows a standard plus a community forum can last. The real cost is keeping records fresh.
  • A layer built only on open standards could register as a Digital Public Good, which helps UN and donor uptake.

Risks

  • Nobody owns accuracy. Every item needs source, date and an expiry. Stale offers are worse than none.
  • Prompt injection. Any contributed text can hide instructions to AI agents. Treat all incoming data as untrusted. Agents only read; they never act on it.
  • Sensitive locations. Needs data can expose vulnerable people. Publish needs only at district level or coarser.
  • Maintenance. Every connector breaks when the other side changes its API. A small team cannot maintain many.

Idea: an AI plugin for a shared index

A plugin people add to the AI assistant they already use (Claude, ChatGPT, Gemini and others). Through it they add their needs and offers to one shared, public index, and find each other.

Why it is strong: it goes where people already work. No new website to visit. The AI does the typing, so adding and renewing a card takes one sentence. That attacks the biggest reason platforms die: stale data.

How it is possible: one MCP server works as a plugin in many assistants. Claude connects to MCP servers. ChatGPT apps are built on MCP. Gemini CLI and most coding tools support it. It is the same server as the agent connector in the section above.

How it works for a user

  1. Add"Add our offer: we run drought cash workshops in Chichewa, Malawi, until March." The AI drafts a card. The user checks it and confirms. The card gets a source, a district-level place and a 60-day expiry.
  2. Find"Who helps with seed distribution in southern Zimbabwe?" The AI searches the index and the open map sources, and shows matches with dates and sources.
  3. Connect"Introduce us." The index sends an intro request through a relay. Contact details are shared only if both sides say yes.
  4. RenewAfter 60 days the AI asks: "Is this still true?" No answer means the card expires.

What the AI search finds

People & groupsWho works on what, where. From the shared index.
Plans & triggersEarly-action plans and trigger rules. From Anticipation Hub, OCHA, local cards.
ResourcesGuides, workshop methods, data, funding calls. From the resource index.
Live signalsForecasts and warnings for a place. From CAP feeds, GDACS, GloFAS, ENSO Outlook.

Every answer shows its source and date. Signals are linked from the official source, never rewritten.

The shared index ("ledger")

  • Cards, not profiles. One need or one offer per card, using the Open Referral fields plus the small "need" format above.
  • Public history. Every change is kept and visible: who added what, when. A plain database with an append-only log or a public git repository does this. No blockchain needed.
  • Open data, open code. Anyone can copy the index or run their own plugin against it.

Why El Niño 2026 has scientists worried · BBC News · The Global Story

  • Climate scientist Tom Di Liberto (Climate Central, former NOAA El Niño forecaster) explains how weaker trade winds shift Pacific thunderstorms and knock over 'dominoes' worldwide.
  • Expected hits: drought and fires in the Philippines, Indonesia and Australia; a weak Indian monsoon; floods in the Horn of Africa after years of drought; drought in Central America, northern South America and the Amazon.
  • Panama declared an El Niño emergency (canal water). Markets move on El Niño forecasts; coffee prices are unlikely to fall.

  • Warning: US famine early warning (USAID) is gone and NOAA staff cuts have hurt public trust in forecasts. Advice: plan now, in the off-season (emergency kit, evacuation routes).

This El Niño Is About To Cause a Global Catastrophe · New Scientist · The World, The Universe And Us

  • Pacific waters may reach +4 °C above average in places, far beyond the previous record. 2027 is almost certain to be the hottest year on record.
  • Climate Impact Lab (University of Chicago) projects about 450,000 extra heat deaths in the Southern Hemisphere over six months (moderate scenario), with a second wave likely in India and Pakistan next spring. Famine, fire, disease and floods come on top.
  • El Niño is natural, but a warmer world amplifies its droughts, floods and storms: it 'loads the dice'.
  • California faces El Niño storms plus a peak in the 18-year king-tide cycle and has declared a pre-emptive state of emergency. The Amazon faces a severe rain deficit near its tipping point.
  • Scientists propose emergency ocean measures: cut fishing quotas now and possibly test local marine cloud brightening over reefs. Idea floated: an ocean-wide state of emergency under the new High Seas Treaty.

ClimateWatch: El Niño strengthens, October update · WeatherWatchTV (New Zealand)

  • Niño-3.4 at +2.72 °C against a +0.8 °C threshold, and still strengthening. Peak around November, staying high to the end of January, easing in February–March.
  • Seas up to 7 °C above normal off Peru and Ecuador; cooler than usual around Fiji, Vanuatu, New Caledonia and New Zealand, so fewer cyclones in the western Pacific.
  • Indian Ocean Dipole neutral but leaning positive; Southern Annular Mode neutral after a stormy, negative September.
  • Drying is showing in New Zealand (Northland to Hawke's Bay) and Queensland; Fiji, Tonga and Rarotonga look dry over the next two weeks.
