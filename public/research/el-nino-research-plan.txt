Global Climate Risk Assessment and Operational Response Framework for the 2026–2027 El Niño Event
Executive Summary
The 2026–2027 El Niño has intensified into a historic super event, with relative ocean temperature anomalies in the Niño 3.4 region reaching +3.0°C by September 2026, indicating a greater than 90% probability of persisting through early 20271.
Record-high global sea surface temperatures have accelerated ocean-atmosphere coupling, driving extreme planetary climate anomalies against a backdrop of existing geopolitical instability and economic fragility2.
East Africa faces a severe bi-phasic hazard profile, experiencing acute drought conditions across central and northern Ethiopia through September 2026, followed by flood-inducing rainfall during the October–December season6.
Southern Africa is entering a highly erratic 2026–2027 agricultural season characterized by severe rainfall deficits, threatening widespread crop failure, pasture depletion, and energy shortages across Zambia, Zimbabwe, and Mozambique8.
Central America’s Dry Corridor is experiencing severe rainfall deficits and dry spells of up to 45 days, prompting a US$60 million United Nations Flash Appeal for Honduras to assist 538,000 vulnerable individuals12.
International appeals have scaled rapidly, led by the International Organization for Migration’s US$110 million appeal and the joint Food and Agriculture Organization–World Food Programme Anticipatory Action Appeal targeting 9 million people across 22 countries14.
Critical funding gaps persist across civil society and country-level response plans, with the United Nations Children's Fund reporting a 55% deficit in preparedness resources for Latin America and the Caribbean16.
Last-mile operational success remains severely constrained by top-down warning systems, bureaucratic hurdles blocking direct funding to local groups, and a lack of hyper-local agronomic decision support8.
Second-order cascading risks—including vector-borne disease surges, reduced hydroelectric output, staple food export restrictions, school dropouts, and heightened gender-based violence—are exacerbating primary climate shocks19.
Nordic institutions are actively deploying early action capital, highlighted by the Norwegian Agency for Development Cooperation’s NOK 30 million allocation to anticipatory trust funds and a structural overhaul of grant schemes to prioritize Southern civil society18.
Ocean-Atmosphere Forecast and Climatological Dynamics
The 2026–2027 El Niño event represents one of the most powerful tropical Pacific ocean-warming events recorded in modern climatology24. The National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA) Climate Prediction Center (CPC) maintained an active El Niño Advisory throughout mid-2026, confirming in its August 13 and September 10 discussions that a very strong event was underway with a near 100% chance of persisting into the Northern Hemisphere spring of 20271. By mid-September 2026, the traditional Niño 3.4 index reached a weekly anomaly of +3.0°C to +3.05°C above the 1991–2020 baseline, climbing faster than during any previously observed event2. Subsurface oceanic heat content across the central and eastern equatorial Pacific exhibited extreme positive anomalies, reaching up to +8.0°C to +10.0°C at depth (50–150 meters), providing a substantial subsurface energy reservoir to sustain surface warming through winter 2026–20271. Coastal sea surface temperature anomalies in the Niño 1+2 region remained persistently above +4.0°C, triggering severe marine heatwaves along the South American Pacific coastline2.
The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) issued global seasonal climate updates in July, August, and September 2026, emphasizing that the ongoing event is developing under unprecedented planetary conditions marked by record global ocean surface temperatures3. Atmosphere-ocean coupling is fully established across the tropical Pacific basin2. The Southern Oscillation Index (SOI) reached −14.6 in August 2026 and plummeted further to a 30-day value of −20.1 by mid-September 2026, signaling a profound collapse of the equatorial Walker Circulation2. Low-level trade winds have weakened or reversed across the central and western Pacific, while upper-level easterly anomalies further confirm strong atmospheric coupling1.
The Australian Bureau of Meteorology (BoM) declared the official onset of El Niño on June 16, 202630. In its Climate Driver Update released on September 27, 2026, the Bureau reported that the Relative Niño 3.4 index reached +2.72°C, standing as the second-highest weekly value recorded by the Bureau since 2008, trailing only the peak of the 2015–2016 event3. International dynamic climate model ensembles monitored by the Bureau forecast additional equatorial Pacific warming through late spring and early summer 2026, with a high probability of exceeding historical sea surface temperature peaks recorded since 19503.
The Indian Ocean Dipole (IOD) presents a vital compounding climate driver4. A positive Indian Ocean Dipole established itself in late August and September 2026, with the Dipole Mode Index reading +0.76°C by late September 20263. Predictive modeling by the World Meteorological Organization and regional climate centers indicates that positive Indian Ocean Dipole conditions will peak during October–November 2026 before decaying in early 202728. When aligned with a super El Niño, a positive Indian Ocean Dipole acts as a powerful climate multiplier: it severely amplifies drought, high temperatures, and fire risks across southern Australia and Southern Africa while simultaneously driving torrential, flood-inducing rains across the Greater Horn of Africa6.
Regional Impact Profiles, Preparation, and Governance
Latin America and the Caribbean
Central America's Dry Corridor—specifically Honduras, El Salvador, and Guatemala—is experiencing one of its most severe droughts in decades12. Extended dry spells (canícula) spanning up to 45 consecutive days without rain have devastated primary crop cycles13. The forecast for the secondary planting season (postrera) indicates the driest conditions recorded since 1991, leading over 60% of surveyed farmers to reduce planted acreage12. Water rationing is widely enforced, with rural communities receiving piped water as infrequently as once per week13. In South America, Pacific coastal nations prepare for heavy rainfall, flash flooding, and landslides along coastal Ecuador, Peru, and Colombia as El Niño peaks in late 202617. Concurrently, the Caribbean Institute for Meteorology and Hydrology (CIMH) has issued emergency drought alerts for Dominica, Saint Lucia, and the Lesser Antilles, where surface water reservoirs are severely depleted13.
Governments across the region have activated national emergency structures12. Honduras declared a state of emergency across 75 municipalities in its red-alert drought zone, home to 2.7 million people12. Guatemala committed US60 million Flash Appeal for Honduras in late September 2026, complementing Central American Central Emergency Response Fund (CERF) anticipatory action allocations active since early 202612. Civil society organizations, including the Honduran Red Cross, national peasant federations, and local faith networks, are driving grassroots relief, though direct financial flows to non-governmental actors remain highly constrained20.

Sub-Saharan Africa
Sub-Saharan Africa is subject to opposing hydro-climatic extremes5. The Greater Horn of Africa faced severe heat and moisture deficits across central and northern Ethiopia, South Sudan, and Uganda through September 20266. However, as the October–December rainy season begins, the combination of El Niño and a positive Indian Ocean Dipole is driving intense, above-normal rainfall and extreme riverine flooding across Somalia, Kenya, southern Ethiopia, Burundi, and Uganda6. Conversely, Southern Africa—including Zambia, Zimbabwe, Mozambique, Malawi, and South Africa—is bracing for an extended summer drought8. High temperatures and delayed rainfall onset threaten to shorten the growing season and reduce staple maize production8. In West Africa, the Regional Climate Centre (AGRHYMET) highlights expanding drought risks across the Western Sahel (e.g., Chad), compounding baseline conflict-driven food insecurity affecting over 52 million people5.
Regional governance bodies have mobilized technical frameworks8. The Intergovernmental Authority on Development (IGAD) Climate Prediction and Applications Centre (ICPAC) convened the 74th Greater Horn of Africa Climate Outlook Forum (GHACOF74) in Kigali in August 2026, launching its first climate-and-health early warning initiative to manage extreme heat and disease threats22. SADC’s Climate Services Centre issued seasonal advisories warning of below-normal rainfall across southern territories11. Governments in Zambia and Zimbabwe are promoting conservation agriculture (Pfumvudza/Intwasa) and urging delayed planting to prevent crop loss from false seasonal starts8. Local civil society networks—such as Youth for ED (YECODE) in Zimbabwe, farmer nurseries in Choma (Zambia), national Red Cross societies, and local faith networks—are disseminating adaptation guidance, but struggle with limited capital access8.

Asia and the Pacific
In South Asia, the India Meteorological Department (IMD) reported that the 2026 southwest monsoon concluded with an overall 13% rainfall deficit characterized by highly erratic, unorganized precipitation patterns4. Below-normal rainfall is forecast across most of India for the October–December post-monsoon period, threatening winter crop (rabi) sowing39. Southeast Asia faces suppressed convection and drought conditions across Indonesia, the Philippines, and Papua New Guinea, though severe localized extreme weather produced flooding that forced Bangkok, Thailand, to declare a state of disaster across all 50 city districts in late September 20262. In the Pacific, 15 Pacific Island Countries have officially declared El Niño status, with Papua New Guinea experiencing widespread water shortages and agricultural disruption34.
National meteorology agencies and regional monitoring structures, such as the ASEAN Specialised Meteorological Centre (ASMC), maintain active warning advisories32. In Papua New Guinea, the National Disaster Centre operates alongside United Nations Disaster Management Teams to coordinate emergency water deliveries34. Civil society action across Asia and the Pacific relies heavily on community-based disaster risk management committees, farmer groups, and local Red Cross Red Crescent networks21.

Financial Landscape, Appeals, and Resource Allocation
Multilateral funding appeals have scaled significantly in response to the growing intensity of El Niño 2026–202712. On September 3, 2026, the International Organization for Migration (IOM) issued a Global El Niño Appeal seeking US$110 million to protect 4.9 million vulnerable, displaced, and mobile individuals across Asia-Pacific, Africa, Latin America, and the Middle East through July 202714. The Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and the World Food Programme (WFP) released a Joint Anticipatory Action Appeal (June 2026–March 2027) targeting nearly 9 million food-insecure people across 22 high-risk countries to safeguard agricultural production and prevent emergency asset sales15.
At the country level, humanitarian appeals face substantial resource constraints12. The United Nations system launched a US24.3 million was available out of required emergency programming funds, leaving a 55% funding gap (US$29.5 million)16.

Governance over resource allocation remains heavily concentrated within multilateral platforms, including UN-managed Country-Based Pooled Funds, the Central Emergency Response Fund, and Red Cross Disaster Response Emergency Fund mechanisms12. While these mechanisms efficiently pool international donor capital, direct access for local civil society organizations remains low18. Local non-governmental organizations frequently operate as downstream subcontractors, incurring administrative delays of three to six months while funds pass through UN agencies and international non-governmental organizations18.
Local Operational Gaps and Last-Mile Vulnerabilities
Consultations with local civil society actors, agricultural extension workers, and community leaders highlight systemic operational gaps in early warning and early action delivery8. National meteorological bulletins regularly communicate probabilistic forecasts—such as a 75% chance of below-normal rainfall—that fail to inform practical farm management8. Smallholder farmers lack precise advice regarding specific planting dates, seed variety selection (such as choosing short-season or drought-tolerant sorghum), and techniques to avoid false seasonal starts8. Major seasonal forecasts are published predominantly in official national languages (English, Spanish, French) and distributed via digital portals, while remote rural populations, indigenous communities, and marginalized pastoralists rely on local community radio broadcasts in regional dialects42.
Direct financial access bottlenecks remain severe18. Local community-based organizations and faith networks possess the trust and operational footprint required for early action, but face stringent compliance, auditing, and institutional requirements that prevent them from accessing pooled humanitarian funds directly18. Furthermore, macro-level climate models fail to capture microclimate anomalies, such as localized valley frost hollows, groundwater table depletion rates, and subnational grain market price spikes17. Local actors lack simple tools to link climate anomalies with subnational market monitoring and acute malnutrition metrics17. Inflexible trigger protocols compound these issues, as pre-arranged anticipatory funding mechanisms often require rigid, multi-point meteorological threshold confirmations before capital is released35. Consequently, funds are frequently disbursed only after dry spells have already damaged germinating crops, missing the critical window for preventative action8.
Second-Order Cascading Impacts and Multi-Sectoral Risks
The 2026–2027 El Niño generates severe cascading multi-sectoral impacts that extend beyond direct agricultural losses5. The collapse of the tropical Walker Circulation and the alignment of a positive Indian Ocean Dipole drive dual pathways of destruction2. Across Southern Africa, Central America, and Southeast Asia, severe drought and extreme heat trigger crop failure and nighttime frost damage, leading to staple price inflation and export restrictions8. Concurrently, reservoir depletion causes severe hydropower rationing and industrial power cuts, while water source contamination leads to cholera outbreaks and heightened risks of gender-based violence at water collection points13. Across East Africa and Pacific Latin America, torrential flooding escalates vector breeding to drive dengue and malaria epidemics, while infrastructure destruction causes school dropouts and forced displacement7.
Public health systems face acute stress21. Elevated temperatures and altered rainfall patterns trigger explosive vector breeding cycles21. Severe dengue outbreaks threaten Latin America and Southeast Asia, while flood-affected zones in East Africa face malaria spikes and waterborne cholera outbreaks caused by damaged sanitation infrastructure13. Unprecedented extreme temperatures led IGAD ICPAC to issue dedicated extreme-heat early warnings in August 2026 to prevent thermal mortality among vulnerable populations22. Severe, multi-season drought across Southern Africa (affecting the Zambezi Basin and Kariba Dam) and South America severely depletes hydroelectric reservoirs, causing industrial power cuts, reduced economic output, and increased reliance on fossil-fuel backup generation.
Agricultural production is further threatened by unseasonal ground frosts19. Unusually dry soils during El Niño periods accelerate overnight radiative heat loss, triggering severe ground frosts in high-altitude and southern agricultural belts19. In Australia, severe frost warnings were issued for cereal crops (wheat, barley, oats) during sensitive flowering stages19. Reduced crop yields combined with panic buying drive rapid staple food price inflation, risking national export bans that further destabilize international grain markets13. In marine ecosystems, ocean heatwaves along South America’s Pacific coast impair the Peruvian anchoveta fishery, disrupting the global supply of fishmeal and fertilizers2. Land-based drought in key producing regions negatively affects global coffee, cocoa, and sugar production.
Infrastructure, education, and security risks are compounding5. Prolonged drought across the Gatun Lake watershed reduces draft limits in the Panama Canal, constraining daily vessel transits, elevating global maritime freight rates, and delaying international supply chains. Water scarcity forces women and young girls to travel longer distances to fetch water, increasing their exposure to gender-based violence43. Children drop out of school in growing numbers to assist families with water hauling, livestock driving, or informal labor43. Furthermore, drought-induced pasture loss alters traditional pastoral transhumance routes in conflict-prone areas, such as the Sahel, Lake Chad Basin, and Liptako-Gourma5. Increased competition over scarce water and grazing land acts as a direct conflict multiplier, increasing local violence and driving secondary internal displacement5.
Synthesis of Historical Lessons and Early Action Failures
Evaluations of major historical events—including the 1997–1998 [OLDER], 2015–2016 [OLDER], and 2023–2024 [OLDER] El Niño cycles—demonstrate repeating structural failures in translating climate alerts into timely mitigation31. Historical evaluations consistently reveal that early warnings rarely lead to early funding35. In 1997–1998 [OLDER] and 2015–2016 [OLDER], official warnings were issued months in advance, yet the international community delayed major capital deployments until acute malnutrition and starvation were documented35. In 2026, climate signals were stable as early as February, proving that any delay in protective action stems from institutional inertia rather than forecasting limitations35. The Red Cross Red Crescent Climate Centre noted on September 28, 2026, that the forecast is as good as forecasting gets, and every day of delay is an institutional choice rather than a gap in knowledge35.
A secondary historical failure involves the false onset of seasonal rains8. In 2015–2016 [OLDER] and 2023–2024 [OLDER], thousands of smallholders planted crops following early, isolated rains, only to lose their seeds when dry spells resumed8. Current 2026 advisories in Southern Africa explicitly focus on preventing premature planting, emphasizing that actionable agricultural guidance must accompany weather warnings8. Finally, international funding surges reactively during emergency relief phases but vanishes rapidly during early recovery, leaving affected communities vulnerable to recurring climate shocks18.
Strategic Value Addition for Outside Groups and Localisation Principles
Outside groups offering technology, artificial intelligence capabilities, and facilitation support must adhere strictly to established Grand Bargain localization commitments and Do-No-Harm principles18. External actors must refrain from creating parallel coordination structures, poaching experienced local staff, or introducing complex, proprietary software that creates long-term vendor dependency18. Top-down technological interventions that operate without local community validation risk disseminating unverified or culturally inappropriate warnings that exacerbate panic8. Small, specialized external groups add genuine value by providing non-bureaucratic support directly to local civil society actors18.
Concrete Pathways to Add Value
AI-Powered Local Language Agricultural Translation Workflows
Primary Beneficiaries: Smallholder farmer cooperatives, community radio stations, and local agricultural extension officers in Central America and the SADC region8.
Operational Delivery: Develop open-source, offline-capable artificial intelligence workflows that convert complex probabilistic climate bulletins into localized agronomic advisories in indigenous and regional dialects8.
Resource Cost: Low-to-medium software engineering time and local translator validation costs.
Risk of Harm: Misinterpretation of probabilistic data causing erroneous farming decisions; mitigated by requiring mandatory validation from local national agronomic institutes8.
Direct Flexible Micro-Granting for Local Anticipatory Action
Primary Beneficiaries: Grassroots non-governmental organizations, community water boards, and women's networks in red-alert drought zones13.
Operational Delivery: Establish rapid-disbursement micro-grant channels (US20,000) that bypass complex multilateral sub-granting procedures, delivering capital directly to local groups within 48 hours of trigger activation18.
Resource Cost: Direct capital funding pool and lightweight administrative compliance management.
Risk of Harm: Financial misallocation or internal community friction; mitigated by utilizing trusted local civil networks as fiscal sponsors20.
Hyper-Local Climate-Market Data Integration Hubs
Primary Beneficiaries: Municipal emergency task forces, local Red Cross chapters, and district-level decision-makers17.
Operational Delivery: Facilitate open-source data integration connecting satellite soil moisture metrics with subnational grain market prices and water table indicators17.
Resource Cost: Data engineering support and dashboard facilitation training.
Risk of Harm: Data overload that confuses local operational planning; mitigated by co-designing simplified, single-indicator risk displays with municipal managers.
Horizontal South-South Peer Facilitation Workshops
Primary Beneficiaries: Farmer cooperatives and community disaster managers across Southern Africa and Central America8.
Operational Delivery: Facilitate virtual and hybrid peer-to-peer knowledge exchanges linking communities experiencing El Niño shocks (such as sharing Zimbabwean Pfumvudza conservation agriculture techniques with Zambian farmer groups)8.
Resource Cost: Facilitation expertise, interpretation services, and digital connectivity support.
Risk of Harm: Contextual mismatch of agricultural techniques; mitigated by ensuring local agronomists lead technical exchanges.
Community Radio Alert Integration Tools
Primary Beneficiaries: Isolated rural and indigenous populations lacking internet connectivity42.
Operational Delivery: Supply low-cost technical bridging tools and automated audio conversion scripts that allow community radio stations to ingest weather advisories and broadcast them as standardized audio updates42.
Resource Cost: Hardware bridge deployment and radio station technical training.
Risk of Harm: Broadcast of inaccurate warnings causing unnecessary panic; mitigated by establishing strict broadcast approval protocols with local authorities.
Nordic Institutional Engagement and Funding Mechanisms
Nordic governments and non-governmental organizations maintain a well-funded, highly active posture regarding climate adaptation and anticipatory action for El Niño 2026–202723. On July 3, 2026, the Norwegian Agency for Development Cooperation (Norad) allocated NOK 30 million explicitly for El Niño anticipatory action, divided evenly between FAO's Special Fund for Emergency and Rehabilitation Activities (SFERA) (NOK 15 million) and WFP's Anticipatory Action Trust Fund (NOK 15 million)23. This targeted funding complements Norway’s overall 2026 humanitarian budget of nearly NOK 6 billion managed by Norad and the Ministry of Foreign Affairs41.
Crucially, Norad announced a major restructuring of its Civil Society Strengthening grant scheme on September 10, 202618. Designed to shift power and resources directly to Southern actors, the new policy mandates that future grant distributions require equitable partnerships and direct local leadership18. An open consultation on the new grant design will take place in December 2026, followed by a call for proposals in early 202718. In parallel, Norwegian Church Aid (NCA) intensified its operational preparedness in late September 2026 across East and Southern Africa, deploying early warning systems, securing emergency stockpiles, and strengthening water, sanitation, and food security infrastructure alongside local faith actors42. NORCAP continues deploying technical climate and protection experts across UN agencies, while the Norwegian Red Cross supports disaster emergency funds, and met.no (Norwegian Meteorological Institute) and CICERO provide climate modeling and regional forecasting support41.

Unknown Unknowns and Blind Spots in El Niño Preparedness
Systemic risks and non-linear dynamics overlooked in standard El Niño preparedness assessments include:
Record Baseline Ocean Heat Coupling: The 2026–2027 El Niño is unfolding on top of unprecedented, record-breaking global ocean temperatures recorded throughout 2025 [OLDER] and 20262. Historical ENSO analogs (1997–1998 [OLDER], 2015–2016 [OLDER]) may fail to accurately predict modern atmospheric responses, leading to unmodeled, extreme compound weather events4.
Radiative Soil Drying and Unseasonal Frost Spikes: Standard climate assessments focus heavily on daytime heat and seasonal rainfall totals while overlooking nighttime radiative freezing risks19. Dry, parched soils lose thermal inertia, creating extreme frost conditions in high-altitude and temperate farm zones that can wipe out flowering crops before severe drought is officially declared19.
Sovereign Debt Distress Limiting Domestic Subsidies: Many developing nations are experiencing severe sovereign debt distress, restricting their ability to fund domestic food, fuel, and fertilizer subsidies5. This macro-economic buffer loss transfers the impact of market price shocks directly onto vulnerable households.
AI Data Center Infrastructure and Water Resource Competition: The rapid expansion of energy-intensive artificial intelligence data centers in water-stressed urban and peri-urban areas introduces an unmodeled structural competitor for municipal groundwater during severe El Niño droughts.
Cascading Green Energy Grid Vulnerabilities: Regions undergoing rapid green energy transitions face simultaneous, compounding grid failures as drought reduces hydroelectric generation while extreme heat and atmospheric haze impair solar panel efficiency and transmission capacity.
Geopolitical Grain Protectionism and Trade Disruption: Elevated international geopolitical friction increases the likelihood that major food-producing nations will enforce sudden export bans on rice, wheat, or maize at the first sign of domestic yield deficits, triggering rapid global food price spikes27.
Climate-Conflict Feedback Loops in Security-Compromised Zones: In fragile regions like the Sahel, Lake Chad, and Eastern Democratic Republic of the Congo, El Niño drought shifts traditional pastoral transhumance routes, causing pastoralists to move into settled agricultural zones and directly triggering inter-communal violence5. Climate shocks act as a force multiplier for non-state armed groups seeking to recruit displaced youth5.
